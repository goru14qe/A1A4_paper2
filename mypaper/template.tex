\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Packed beds are widely used as catalytic reactors, adsorbers, and heat and mass-transfer devices in chemical, energy, and environmental process engineering~\citep{Dixon,Aglave}. Their performance including conversion, selectivity, pressure drop, and thermal management is governed not only by the bulk porosity but also by the organization of the pore-scale flow, which determines local residence times and interphase heat and mass transfer~\citep{Augier,freund2003numerical}. Conventional continuum models based on Darcy's law or volume averaging replace the complex pore structure with effective transport coefficients and therefore are unable to capture key pore-scale flow phenomena, including jetting, channeling, and recirculation, which dominate interstitial mixing. Resolving the flow at the scale of the individual pores is therefore a prerequisite for understanding, and ultimately designing, how such beds perform.\\
Conventional beds of randomly poured particles offer little control over this pore-scale flow: the void network is statistically reproducible but geometrically uncontrolled, so the flow organisation cannot be tuned deliberately. Structured and additively manufactured packings change this premise by fixing the pore geometry by design, turning the internal topology into an engineering variable~\citep{Larsson.2018,li2022visualization}. The rotationally stacked, modular bed studied here is a deliberate instance of this idea: identical bar modules are stacked and each successive layer is rotated by a fixed angle relative to the one below, so that a single rotation increment acts as a design knob that reshapes the entire interstitial pore network. When the rotation angle is commensurate with the $180^\circ$ symmetry of the bar pattern, the stack becomes geometrically periodic over a finite number of layers (six layers at $30^\circ$, nine at $40^\circ$) and the pores trace a spiral path through the bed; an aperiodic, irregularly rotated stack provides a geometrically disordered counterpart.

This configuration has been experimentally characterized by means of optically corrected, refractive-index-matched particle image velocimetry, which resolves the time-averaged velocity field within the slits of the upper layers~\citep{velten2026,Martins.2018,velten2022a}; related PIV and tomographic studies have established such measurements as a reliable pore-scale reference in both random and ordered packings~\citep{Patil.2013,Khayamyan.2017,Poelma.2020}. A recurring qualitative observation across this and earlier computational work is that, in the periodically rotated beds, the flow appears to repeat from one geometric period to the next: successive layers seem to carry the same interstitial flow pattern, merely rotated with the geometry\citep{demir2026pressure}. This layer-to-layer repeatability has been noted in passing but never defined, quantified, or tested against a disordered control, and it has not been examined across more than one rotation\citep{sadowski2025mean}.

Beyond these macroscopic observations, however, the underlying organisation of the pore-scale flow remains largely unexplored. Although successive layers were observed to exhibit similar velocity fields rotated with the packing geometry, this behaviour has only been noted qualitatively. Its physical origin, robustness across different rotational topologies, and distinction from the behaviour of non-periodic packings have not yet been investigated systematically.
Despite recent progress, the influence of rotational topology on pore-scale flow organisation remains poorly understood. Although recurring velocity patterns have been observed in successive packing layers, they have not been quantitatively characterised, nor has it been established whether they arise from the periodic geometry itself. In this work, we investigate this phenomenon using an experimentally validated lattice Boltzmann model, introduce the concept of flow locking to describe the recurrence of pore-scale flow structures, and quantify its dependence on packing topology through comparisons with an irregular configuration.

To address these questions, the present work combines pore-scale lattice Boltzmann simulations~\citep{kruger2017lattice,Sullivan} with particle image velocimetry measurements for three packing topologies: the periodic $30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ stacks and an irregular configuration. The simulations reproduce the complete bounded experimental apparatus and are validated against layer-resolved velocity measurements. Building on this validated framework, we introduce the concept of flow locking to describe the topology-induced recurrence of pore-scale flow structures, quantify it using objective similarity metrics, and investigate its dependence on packing topology and Reynolds number. By comparing periodic and irregular packings, the study establishes the role of rotational periodicity in governing the pore-scale flow organization and provides a physical basis for the use of reduced periodic computational domains.

\subsection{Twist-and-Repeat Topology}
\label{sec:geometry:topology}

Because the modules are identical and only their angular offset changes, the bed is generated by a single \emph{twist-and-repeat} rule: each module is the one below it, rotated about the column axis by a fixed angle $\theta$ and shifted up by one layer height $l_\mathrm{ch}$. The structure therefore possesses two competing geometric length scales. The first is the per-layer misalignment $\theta$, which controls how far an open slit (and hence a through-flowing jet) stays aligned before it is occluded by the bars of the next module. The second is a superlattice period: since the bar pattern of a single module is invariant under a $180^\circ$ rotation, the stack returns to its original orientation after
\begin{equation}
    N = \frac{180^\circ}{\theta}
    \label{eq:period}
\end{equation}
layers. For the regular $30^\circ$ configuration ($\theta = 30^\circ$) gives $N = 6$, so six successive modules span and the seventh module repeats the orientation of the first~\citep{velten2026}. The $40^\circ$ configuration gives the longer period $N = 9$. The full bed of $18$ stacked modules (bed height $18\,l_\mathrm{ch} = 180\,\mathrm{mm}$) thus contains exactly three complete geometric periods of the $30^\circ$ packing, producing the spiral inner pore structure illustrated in \hl{Fig(to be added)}.~\ref{fig:geometry}. All optical measurements~\citep{velten2026}, and \hl{the simulation planes (really??) }used for validation here, are taken in layers L13-L17, i.e.\ within the third (fully developed) period counted from the gas inlet at the bottom of the bed.

A consequence of the $180^\circ$ symmetry of the bar pattern is that the inner pore
network is point-symmetric between two layers: when two modules are rotated against each other, the first and fourth voids carry the same number of inlets as the second and third, and depending on $\theta$ between one and four through-passages connect a lower module to the one above it~\citep{velten2026}. The $30^\circ$ configuration realises a $2/3$ inlet pattern.

\subsection{Topology Configurations}
\label{sec:geometry:topologies}

The modularity of the reactor allows a continuum of pore structures to be assembled
from the same hardware simply by choosing the inter-layer rotation angle~\citep{velten2026}. We study three representative topologies here:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$\theta = 30^\circ$ (regular, period $N = 6$):} the experimentally measured benchmark configuration, with the shortest period and the longest jet-alignment length of the cases considered.
    \item \textbf{$\theta = 40^\circ$ (regular, period $N = 9$):} a second periodic packing whose larger per-layer twist shortens the alignment length and lengthens the geometric period.
    \item \textbf{Irregular (aperiodic control):} successive layers are rotated by a non-repeating sequence of angles, so there is no twist-and-repeat rule and no
    superlattice period. This packing serves as a clean control: it shares the same bars, slits, porosity and bounding apparatus as the regular cases but lacks the geometric periodicity, and therefore cannot exhibit flow locking (Sec.~\ref{sec:locking}).
\end{itemize}

Simulations at $Re_P = 100$ and $Re_P = 300$ are performed for all three packing topologies ($30^\circ$, $40^\circ$, and the irregular configuration), as corresponding PIV measurements are available for these conditions and provide the primary validation cases (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}).

\section{Numerical solver: the lattice Boltzmann method}
\label{sec:numerics}
\subsection{Lattice Boltzmann Formulation (ALBORZ)}
\label{sec:numerics:lbm}

The flow simulations presented here are performed using the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM), which may be regarded as a discretization on the kinetic-scale of the Boltzmann equation that recovers the Navier-Stokes equations in the hydrodynamic limit~\cite{Xiaoyi}. The numerical solver and formulation adopted here are identical to those previously developed and validated against packed-bed experiments by the present author~\cite{neeraj2023}; readers are referred to that work for a comprehensive description. Briefly, the method begins with a projection of the Boltzmann equation onto a discrete velocity space, whereby the particle distribution function is approximated through a truncated Hermite polynomial expansion. Integration of the resulting system of coupled hyperbolic equations along their characteristic trajectories~\cite{Bhadauria} yields the well-known two-step stream-and-collide algorithm:
\begin{equation}
    f_i\left(\bm{r}+\bm{c}_i\delta t, t+\delta t\right) - f_i\left(\bm{r}, t\right) = \Omega_i,
\end{equation}
Here the $f_i$ denote the discrete populations advected along the lattice links with velocities $\bm{c}_i$, $\delta t$ is the discrete time increment, and $\Omega_i$ is the collision operator, which drives the populations towards their equilibrium state in mimicry of inter-molecular collisions. For weakly non-equilibrium flows i.e.\ at the low Knudsen numbers characteristic of the present configuration it is common to adopt the single-relaxation-time Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) closure, whose discrete counterpart reads:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i = \omega\left( f^{\rm eq}_i - f_i\right) + \Xi_i,
\end{equation}
in which $\omega$ sets the rate of relaxation towards equilibrium and $f^{\rm eq}_i$ is the discretised equilibrium attractor, i.e.\ the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution recovered as the entropy-minimising state~\cite{Bhadauria,Shu}. The additional term $\Xi_i$ is a correction that compensates for the spurious deviations arising in the diagonal entries of the third-order equilibrium moments whenever a third-order quadrature is used. The equilibrium itself is constructed, as is standard in the method, by truncating the Hermite expansion of the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution at finite order and evaluating it on the Gauss-Hermite quadrature nodes:
\begin{equation}
    f^{\rm eq}_i = w_i \sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{n!c_s^{2n}} \mathcal{H}_n(\bm{c}_i):a_n^{\rm eq}(\rho,\bm{u},p/\rho),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}_n$ denotes the rank-$n$ Hermite polynomial tensor, $a_n^{\rm eq}$ its associated equilibrium coefficient, $w_i$ the quadrature weights and $c_s$ the lattice sound speed fixed by the quadrature; $\rho$, $\bm{u}$ and $p$ are the density, velocity and pressure, and $N$ the truncation order. Throughout this work we employ the D3Q27 lattices ($D$ the spatial dimension, $Q$ the number of links), both of which correspond to a third-order quadrature; the expansion is therefore truncated at $N=4$ in two dimensions and $N=6$ in three, these being the highest orders the respective stencils can support. A virtue of this higher-order construction, in contrast with the conventional second-order equilibrium, is that the shear-mode dissipation rate retains Galilean invariance at the Navier-Stokes level. The complementary correction $\Xi_i$ extends this invariance to the dissipation of the normal modes and is given by~\cite{hosseini2020compressibility,feng2015three}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:correction}
    \Xi_i = \left(1-\frac{\omega}{2}\right)\frac{w_i}{2c_s^4}\left(\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{H}_2\right):\Delta^{\rm eq}_3,
\end{equation}
with $\Delta^{\rm eq}_3$ the rank-three diagonal tensor
\begin{equation}
    \Delta^{\rm eq}_{\alpha\alpha\alpha} = \rho u_\alpha \left(u_\alpha^2 + 3p/\rho\right) - \sum_i c_{i,\alpha}^3 f^{\rm eq}_i.
\end{equation}
In practice the plain BGK operator is frequently replaced by more elaborate collision models, motivated either physically (e.g.\ to recover an arbitrary Prandtl number) or numerically (to enhance stability). A prominent example is the multiple-relaxation-time (MRT) approach, in which the relaxation is performed not on the populations themselves but on a set of independent base functions usually moments of the distribution and which has gained wide adoption thanks to its favourable stability and accuracy. The collision model used here is an MRT operator formulated in a basis of modified central Hermite moments, which in particular permits the bulk viscosity to be tuned independently~\cite{hosseini2022lattice}. Unlike the classical Hermite-moment basis~\cite{hosseini2021central,huang2022simulation}, this choice lets the trace and the deviatoric parts of the second-order moments relax with distinct rates:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i = \mathcal{T}^{-1}W\mathcal{T}(f^{\rm eq}_i - f_i) + \Xi_i\label{eq:correc1}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}$ maps the populations into moment space, $\mathcal{T}^{-1}$ is its inverse and $W$ collects the relaxation rates on its diagonal. The transform $\mathcal{T}$ is built from the modified central Hermite polynomials $\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_n(\bm{c}_i)$, which for the D2Q9 lattice take the form:
\begin{subequations}
	\begin{align}
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_0 &= 1, \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_x &= c_{i,x}-u_x, \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_y &= c_{i,y}-u_y, \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xy} &= (c_{i,x}-u_x) (c_{i,y}-u_y), \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xx-yy} &= {(c_{i,x}-u_x)}^2 - {(c_{i,y}-u_y)}^2, \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xx+yy} &= {(c_{i,x}-u_x)}^2 + {(c_{i,y}-u_y)}^2 - 2c_s^2,\\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xxy} &= {(c_{i,x}-u_x)}^2(c_{i,y}-u_y) - c_s^2(c_{i,y}-u_y),\\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xyy} &= {(c_{i,y}-u_y)}^2(c_{i,x}-u_x) - c_s^2(c_{i,x}-u_x),\\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xxyy} &= \left[{(c_{i,x}-u_x)}^2 - c_s^2\right] \left[{(c_{i,y}-u_y)}^2 - c_s^2\right].
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Projecting the equilibrium onto this basis yields the corresponding central Hermite coefficients $\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_n$, which for the D2Q9 equilibrium are:
\begin{subequations}
	\begin{align}
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_0 &= \rho, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_x &= 0, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_y &= 0, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xy} &= 0, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xx-yy} &= 0, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xx+yy} &= 2(p - \rho c_s^2),\\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xxy} &= 0,\\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xyy} &= 0,\\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xxyy} &= \rho{(p/\rho - c_s^2)}^2.
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
For the particular choice $p=\rho c_s^2$ every coefficient above vanishes except the zeroth-order one. On the D2Q9 lattice the diagonal relaxation tensor $W$ is assembled as:
\begin{equation}
    W={\rm diag}(1, 1, 1, \omega_s, \omega_s, \omega_b, \omega_g, \omega_g, \omega_g),
\end{equation}
where the operator ${\rm diag}$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    {\rm diag}(\bm{A}) = (\bm{A}\otimes\bm{1})\circ \bm{I},
\end{equation}
in which $\bm{A}$ is an arbitrary vector, $\bm{1}$ the all-ones vector, $\bm{I}$ the identity tensor and $\circ$ the Hadamard (element-wise) product. The entries $\omega_s$, $\omega_b$ and $\omega_g$ are the relaxation frequencies of the shear, bulk and ghost modes, respectively. The bulk-mode frequency $\omega_b$ is tied to the bulk viscosity $\eta$ through:
\begin{equation}
    \omega_b = \frac{\delta t}{(\frac{2+D}{D}-\frac{\partial\ln p}{\partial\ln \rho})\frac{\eta}{p} + \delta t/2}.
\end{equation}
Within this multi-relaxation framework the third-order correction $\Xi_i$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:correc1} is recast accordingly, the single frequency $\omega$ being replaced by the harmonic combination of the shear and bulk rates:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:correction_MRT}
    \Xi_i = \left(1-\frac{\omega_b\omega_s}{\omega_b+\omega_s}\right)\frac{w_i}{2c_s^4}\left(\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{H}_2\right):\Delta^{\rm eq}_3.
\end{equation}
Taken together, the formulation outlined above delivers a freely adjustable bulk viscosity while preserving the Galilean invariance of both the dynamic and the bulk viscosities.

\subsection{Computational Domain and Boundary Conditions}
\label{sec:numerics:domain}

While a variety of different boundary conditions have been developed for the lattice Boltzmann method, in the context of the present study all solid boundaries are modeled using the half-way bounce-back scheme where missing populations after the collision-streaming steps are computed as~\cite{kruger2017lattice}:
\begin{equation}
    f_\alpha\left(\bm{x},t+\delta_t\right) = f^{*}_{\bar{\alpha}}\left(\bm{x},t\right),
\end{equation}
where $f^{*}$ is the post-collision population (prior to streaming) and $\bar{\alpha}$ is the index of the particle velocity opposite that of $\alpha$. To get rid of the stair-case approximation the half-way bounce-back is supplemented with the treatment for curved boundaries as proposed in~\mbox{\cite{bouzidi2001momentum}}.

At a given boundary node $\bm{x}_f$, the missing incoming populations are computed as:
\begin{subequations}
	\begin{align}
		f_\alpha(\bm{x}_f, t+\delta_t) &= 2qf_{\bar{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_f+\bm{c}_{\bar{\alpha}}, t+\delta_t)\nonumber \\  &+\left(1-2q\right)f_{\bar{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_f, t+\delta_t), \quad \forall\; q<\frac{1}{2},\\
		f_\alpha(\bm{x}_f, t+\delta_t) &= \frac{1}{2q}f_{\bar{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_f+\bm{c}_{\bar{\alpha}}, t+\delta_t)\nonumber \\  &+\frac{2q-1}{2q}f_{\bar{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_f, t+\delta_t), \quad \forall\; q\geq\frac{1}{2},
		\end{align}
	\label{Eq:CE_moments_eq}
\end{subequations}
where $\bar{\alpha}$ designates the direction opposite $\alpha$ and $q$:
\begin{equation}
    q = \frac{\lvert\lvert \bm{x}_f - \bm{x}_s\lvert\lvert}{\lvert\lvert\bm{c}_\alpha \lvert\lvert},
\end{equation}
with $\bm{x}_s$ denoting the wall position in direction $\alpha$.

Other boundary conditions applied at the inlets and outlets, i.e.\ constant velocity and constant pressure, are realized using the non-equilibrium extrapolation approach proposed in~\cite{zhao2002non}. In this approach the missing populations at the boundary nodes are reconstructed as:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\alpha}\left(\bm{x}_w,t+\delta_t\right) = f^{(eq)}_{\alpha}\left(\bm{x}_w,t+\delta_t\right) + f^{(neq)}_{\alpha}\left(\bm{x}_w,t+\delta_t\right),
\end{equation}
where $\bm{x}_w$ is the position of the boundary node and $f^{(neq)}$ the non-equilibrium part of the distribution function. One of the two variables (density or velocity) is known via the boundary condition and the other is computed by interpolation from neighboring nodes. The non-equilibrium part of the distribution function is also computed via an interpolation in space.

\subsection{Measurement Positions and Coordinate System}
\label{sec:geometry:coords}
A right-handed global Cartesian system $(x,y,z)$ is fixed to the apparatus: $x$ is horizontal, the depth axis $y$ originates at the centre of the module, and the streamwise axis $z$ points upward with its origin at the bottom of the lowest module (Fig.~\ref{fig:coords}\citealt{velten2026}). Since each measurement (or extraction) plane lies in a void at a different rotation angle, an in-plane coordinate system is used in addition: it keeps the same vertical axis $z$ but replaces $x$ with the horizontal in-plane coordinate $\xi$, aligned with the open slit and zero at the void centre. The two directly reported velocity components are the horizontal in-plane velocity $v_\xi$ and the vertical (streamwise) velocity $v_z$.\\
A measurement plane is located in the global frame by its offset $a_{\mathrm{Pos},n}$ from the reactor centre, measured along $y$ for the $0^\circ$ orientation of the module; this offset is independent of the rotation angle. Four positions are defined (Table~\ref{tab:pos}). Because the bed is point-symmetric about the column axis (Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry:topology}), Pos\,1/Pos\,7 and Pos\,3/Pos\,5 form point-symmetric pairs; the experiment exploits this symmetry, measuring only at Pos\,1 and Pos\,3 to recover the full set of flow fields~\citep{velten2026}. The present validation correspondingly extracts simulation planes at Pos\,1 and Pos\,3.

Because the void in which each plane lies has been twisted by the per-layer rule (Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry:topology}), the raw simulation fields are first de-rotated into the common in-plane frame before any comparison or layer-to-layer analysis. Each layer $L$ is rotated about the vertical axis by
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = -\,(18 - L)\,\theta,
    \label{eq:derotation}
\end{equation}
so that the open slit of every layer is aligned with the in-plane axis $\xi$ (e.g.\ for the $30^\circ$ packing $\alpha = 0,\,-30^\circ,\,-60^\circ,\dots$ for $L = 18, 17, 16,\dots$; for the $40^\circ$ packing the step is $40^\circ$). The position coordinates $(x,y,z)$ are transformed by the planar rotation
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:transform}
\begin{align}
    x' &= \phantom{-}x\cos\alpha + y\sin\alpha, \\
    y' &= -x\sin\alpha + y\cos\alpha, \\
    z' &= z,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
and the velocity components $(v_x, v_y, v_z)$ are rotated consistently,
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:transform_vel}
\begin{align}
    v_x' &= v_x\cos\alpha + v_y\sin\alpha, \\
    v_y' &= v_x\sin\alpha + v_y\cos\alpha, \\
    v_z' &= v_z,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with the vertical coordinate and the streamwise velocity left unchanged. The reported in-plane velocity magnitude combines the horizontal in-plane and streamwise components within the plane,
\begin{equation}
    v_\mathrm{mag} = \sqrt{ {v_x'}^2 + {v_z'}^2 }.
    \label{eq:velmag}
\end{equation}
After this transformation the horizontal in-plane component $v_x'$ corresponds to $v_\xi$ and $v_z'$ to $v_z$ of the in-plane frame.

\subsection{Validation against PIV Measurements}\label{sec:validation}
The simulations are validated against the published PIV data of~\citet{velten2026} for all three topologies. This section first defines the comparison procedure and the error metrics, then assesses the time-averaged velocity-magnitude fields over the measurement planes (surface validation); the layer-resolved development of the flow within the slits is examined subsequently.

\subsubsection{Comparison Procedure and Error Metrics}
\label{sec:validation:procedure}

Velocity data are reported in the in-plane coordinate system of~\citet{velten2026}: the horizontal in-plane axis $\xi$ and the vertical streamwise axis $z$. Simulation planes are extracted at the physical positions corresponding to Pos\,1 and Pos\,3 ($a_\mathrm{Pos} = -22.5$ and $-7.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the reactor centre). Each extracted plane is masked to the fluid region, interpolated onto the experimental PIV grid (vector resolution $3.40$-$3.44\,\mathrm{vec/mm}$, Table~2 of \citealt{velten2026}), and compared with the measured field point by point.

Agreement is quantified on the velocity magnitude $|\bm{v}|$ over all $M$ fluid grid points of a plane. Denoting the simulated and experimental values at point $i$ by $s_i$ and $e_i$, we report the root-mean-square error
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(s_i-e_i\right)^2},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{NRMSE} = \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}}{e_\mathrm{max}-e_\mathrm{min}},
    \label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
its value normalised by the experimental dynamic range ($\mathrm{NRMSE}$), and the coefficient of determination $R^2 = 1-\sum_i(s_i-e_i)^2/\sum_i(e_i-\bar{e})^2$, which measures how much of the spatial variance of the measured field is reproduced. $\mathrm{NRMSE}$ is the primary headline metric, as it is insensitive to the absolute velocity level and is therefore comparable across topologies and Reynolds numbers.

\subsubsection{Surface Velocity Fields}
\label{sec:validation:surface}

We first validate the simulation at the level of the time-averaged surface field, where the PIV data are densest and the flow features are most readily compared. The aim is to establish, that the model reproduces both the spatial organisation of the flow and its absolute velocity level over the full measurement plane.

Figure~\ref{fig:validation_surface} compares the simulated and measured velocity-magnitude fields, with in-plane streamlines, for the $30^\circ$ packing at Pos\,3 at $Re_P=100$ and $Re_P=300$. At both Reynolds numbers the flow organises into discrete streamwise jets issuing from the slits and separated by low-velocity wakes, with a strong central jet flanked by weaker slit jets. The simulation recovers the number, position, width, and peak magnitude of these jets, together with the streamline topology of the
intervening recirculation zones; crucially, the agreement is just as close at $Re_P=300$ as at $Re_P=100$, showing that the model remains predictive as the flow strengthens. The agreement is quantified in Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_profiles}, which overlays the simulated and measured velocity-magnitude profiles at four streamwise heights for Pos\,3
against the experimental uncertainty band: the simulated profiles track the measured ones within or close to the band almost everywhere, capturing both the jet peaks and the inter-jet minima.

This level of agreement is representative, across the regular packings the surface fields are reproduced consistently well at both Reynolds numbers,
with $\mathrm{NRMSE}$ of $5$-$9\%$ and $R^2$ up to $0.93$. The residual error is not distributed uniformly but concentrates in the thin, high-gradient shear layers bordering the jets, where sub-vector-spacing velocity gradients and PIV peak-locking are largest.

\subsubsection{Layer-Resolved Development}
\label{sec:validation:layers}

Beyond the surface fields, the PIV data resolve the flow within the individual layers, allowing the simulation to be validated layer by layer in the developed region. The
complete dataset comprises in-slit velocity fields and profiles for every measured layer (L13-L17) of each topology, Reynolds number and measurement position; rather than reproducing all of these contour sets, we present two contrasting cases at $Re_P = 100$, Pos\,3-the regular $30^\circ$ packing and the irregular packing-which together show both the validation accuracy and the qualitatively different layer behaviour of the two arrangements.

For the regular $30^\circ$ packing (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_layers}), the velocity-magnitude
contours and in-plane streamlines (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_layers}a) reproduce the measured
jet-recirculation structure of each layer, the residuals concentrating in thin shear layers at the jet edges where the steepest gradients amplify any small differences between simulation and PIV. The per-layer line profiles at $2\,\mathrm{mm}$ above the lower face
(Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_layers}b) show that the five layers L13-L17 carry essentially the same profile and that the simulation tracks the measured shape in each-the central jet, the flanking secondary jets, and the inter-jet minima all in the right places. A small, systematic offset is however visible: the simulated central peak and the secondary-jet maxima sit a few percent below the measured ones, while the inter-jet minima are correspondingly slightly higher. The two observations are linked and point to a single cause. The slit jets and their bounding shear layers are the steepest-gradient features in the domain, and each jet core is resolved by only a handful of lattice nodes across its width; there the leading truncation error of the scheme acts as a weak numerical dissipation that clips the tip of the jet. Because the total mass flux through the plane is
conserved, the momentum shaved from the peak is not lost but redistributed into the adjacent low-velocity region exactly the signature seen, lower maxima together with raised minima at unchanged jet position and width. A finer lattice would narrow this gap, but at a steep cost
in the present multi-module domain; the chosen resolution already lies in the grid-converged range for the integral quantities (Sec.~\ref{sec:numerics:grid}).
Another factor is the idealised model geometry-perfectly
smooth, aligned bars and a uniform inlet-differs slightly from the printed parts ($0.3\%$ tolerance) and diffuser-fed inlet of the experiment, which can sharpen the real channelling through individual slits. Secondly, the finite PIV interrogation window lightly smooths the measured field (measurement error). The discrepancy is therefore small, spatially confined to the jet maxima, sign-consistent across all five layers and hence also resonates the same in the surface data above. It is well within the combined experimental and numerical uncertainty and it leaves both the validation conclusion and the layer-to-layer repeatability that is the point of this figure intact.

The irregular packing exhibits fundamentally different behaviour from the periodic configurations (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_layers_irreg}). Because each layer has a unique local bar arrangement, the corresponding jet and wake structures are also unique. Unlike the $30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ packings, the flow fields cannot be collapsed onto a common profile without superimposing unrelated features. For this reason, each layer is validated individually against its corresponding PIV measurements (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_layers_irreg}b).

Despite the absence of layer collapse, the numerical model accurately reproduces the measured flow in every layer. The simulations capture the layer-specific jet locations, jet widths, peak velocities, and associated recirculation regions, demonstrating that the irregular packing is validated with the same methodology as the periodic cases. Although the agreement is slightly less precise than for the regular packings, the origin of these differences is geometric rather than numerical. In the periodic configurations, the repeating topology causes any small manufacturing or assembly imperfections to be replicated from one period to the next, so their influence is effectively averaged over the repeated flow structures. In contrast, every layer of the irregular packing possesses a distinct geometry, making the local flow considerably more sensitive to small variations in rotation angle and the manufacturing tolerance of each individual module (approximately $0.3%$). Since the flow is concentrated into isolated channels bounded by strong shear layers (Sec.~\ref{sec:organisation:contrast}), even minor geometric deviations can produce noticeable local velocity differences between the experiments and the idealised numerical geometry.

These discrepancies are therefore expected and do not diminish the validity of the simulations. Rather, the pronounced differences between the layer-wise velocity profiles from L12 to L17 provide direct evidence that flow locking is absent in the irregular topology. This behaviour is also observed at $Re_P=300$, where the simulations again reproduce the distinct flow pattern of each layer in close agreement with the corresponding PIV measurements, confirming that the absence of flow locking is a robust geometric characteristic rather than a low-Reynolds-number effect.

\section{Flow Locking Framework}
\label{sec:locking}

The central, novel contribution of this paper. Prior work~\citep{velten2026} noted
qualitatively that the flow ``repeats across layers and is antisymmetric about the
central axis.'' Here that observation is turned into a measured quantity: the mean
flow repeats with the same twist as the geometry, and we score how strongly with a
single order parameter that approaches unity for the periodic packings, collapses for
the irregular packing, and weakens as the Reynolds number rises.

 \label{sec:locking:concept}

The regular packings obey a simple geometric construction rule: rotating the bed by the
twist angle $\theta$ and shifting it up by one layer maps the structure onto itself.
The slit pattern therefore advances like a screw thread, returning to its starting
orientation every $N = 180^\circ/\theta$ layers ($N=6$ for $\theta=30^\circ$, $N=9$ for
$\theta=40^\circ$; Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry:topology}). We define \emph{flow locking} as
the situation in which the time-mean flow obeys the same rule as the geometry: once the
mean field of a layer is de-rotated by $-n\theta$ back into a common reference frame
(Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry:coords}), every layer collapses onto a single, repeating
pore-scale pattern that simply spirals upward with the slits. A locked flow is thus
fully described by the field within a single layer period.

The irregular packing is built without a twist-and-repeat rule and so provides a clean
control: lacking any geometric period, its de-rotated layers have no reason to coincide,
and any apparent repetition would be coincidental. This contrast lets us test three
hypotheses. (H1)~The regular packings lock with their geometric period $N$. (H2)~The
irregular packing does not lock. (H3)~Locking weakens as the pore Reynolds number $Re_P$
rises and the flow approaches the unsteady regime. The remainder of this section turns
the qualitative ``repeats across layers'' observation of~\citet{velten2026} into the
measured order parameter $L(n)$ and evaluates these three hypotheses in turn.

\subsection{Locking Order Parameter \texorpdfstring{$L(n)$}{L(n)}}
\label{sec:locking:Ln}

The locking order parameter $L(n)$ is the cosine similarity between the de-rotated
velocity fields of layer $n$ and layer $n+N$:
\begin{equation}
    L(n) = \frac{\langle \tilde{\bm{u}}_n \cdot \tilde{\bm{u}}_{n+N} \rangle}
                {\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_n\|\;\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_{n+N}\|},
    \label{eq:Ln}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\bm{u}}_n$ is the time-mean in-plane velocity field of layer $n$,
de-rotated by $-n\theta$ to a common reference frame, and $N = 180^\circ/\theta$ is
the layer period ($N=6$ for $\theta=30^\circ$; $N=9$ for $\theta=40^\circ$).
$L = 1$ means the de-rotated layers are identical (perfectly locked); $L \to 0$ means
no repeat. For the irregular packing, which has no period $N$, adjacent layers
($n \to n+1$) are compared instead, so that any short-range similarity is given the best
possible chance of registering.

In practice each de-rotated layer field is first interpolated onto a common
$(x',z')$ grid spanning the measurement plane, with $z'$ taken layer-local (measured
from the lower face of each $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ slab) so that the layers overlay. Because
the de-rotation maps the irregular footprint of each layer's fluid region slightly
differently, the grid coverage is not identical between layers; $L(n)$ is therefore
evaluated on a \emph{pairwise} mask containing only the grid points that are fluid in
\emph{both} layers being compared. Imposing a single mask common to all layers would
discard most of the plane and is deliberately avoided. The stacked in-plane components
$(\tilde u_{x'}, \tilde u_{z'})$ enter the cosine measure together, so $L$ rewards both
the correct flow magnitude and the correct in-plane direction.

The analysis is restricted to the developed window L13-L17 (the third geometric period
from the inlet), where the layer-to-layer matrix is already flat (Sec.~\ref{sec:locking:topologies});
the mean flow has therefore completed any inlet development and reached its locked state
well before this depth.

\subsection{Antisymmetry Index \texorpdfstring{$A(n)$}{A(n)}}
\label{sec:locking:An}

A locked state should also inherit the point symmetry of the slit arrangement. This can
be probed with an antisymmetry index $A(n)$, the cosine similarity between the de-rotated
field and its reflection about the column axis (transverse component reversed, streamwise
component mirrored). For the regular packings $A$ is only moderate ($A \approx 0.4$-$0.5$):
the cosine measure is dominated by the strong, near-symmetric streamwise jet, so a single
in-plane plane samples the axial symmetry only indirectly. Because the point symmetry is
already established directly and far more convincingly by the Pos\,3$\leftrightarrow$Pos\,5
mirror agreement (Sec.~\ref{sec:numerics:symmetry}), we lead the locking argument with
$L(n)$ and retain $A(n)$ only as a minor supporting diagnostic.

\subsection{Locking Across Topologies}
\label{sec:locking:topologies}

The locking visual of Fig.~\ref{fig:lockvis} makes the effect immediately apparent. It
shows the de-rotated velocity-magnitude fields and in-plane streamlines of all fifteen
layers of the $30^\circ$ packing at $Re_P=100$ and $Re_P=300$. Once each layer is rotated
back into the common frame, the layers are nearly indistinguishable: the same central
high-velocity jet, the same pair of flanking slit jets, and the same recirculation pockets
recur in every layer, simply translated upward with the spiralling slits. The pattern
holds at both Reynolds numbers, confirming the locked state qualitatively; the slightly
greater layer-to-layer variation visible at $Re_P=300$ anticipates the Reynolds-number
weakening quantified in Sec.~\ref{sec:locking:reynolds}.

This impression is quantified in Fig.~\ref{fig:Ln}, which shows the layer-to-layer
locking matrix $L$ and the per-layer locking $L(n)$ against the across-layer mean for the
three topologies.
For the $30^\circ$ packing every off-diagonal entry is $L \approx 1.000$ (lowest
$\approx 0.997$) and $L(n) \approx 0.998$ for all layers, confirming near-perfect locking
across the developed window L13-L17 (Fig.~\ref{fig:Ln}a). This is corroborated by the
per-layer mean speed, which varies by only a coefficient of variation of $1.4\%$ across
the fifteen layers at Pos\,3 ($1.8\%$ at Pos\,1)-the layers are statistically
interchangeable.
The $40^\circ$ packing locks almost as strongly, with off-diagonal $L \approx 0.99$
(range $0.987$-$1.000$) and $L(n) \ge 0.99$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:Ln}b); the slight
reduction is consistent with the larger per-layer twist angle, which redistributes more
momentum across the plane between successive layers.
The irregular packing behaves completely differently (Fig.~\ref{fig:Ln}c): off-diagonal
entries scatter erratically between $L = 0.10$ and $0.68$ with no systematic structure,
and $L(n)$ against the mean ranges from $0.66$ to $0.83$ without converging. Even though
adjacent rather than period-$N$ layers are compared-the most generous possible
test-no repeating pattern emerges.
The contrast confirms hypotheses~H1 and~H2: the geometric twist-and-repeat rule is a
necessary condition for flow locking, and removing it abolishes the layer-periodic
organisation entirely. The irregular case thus acts as a clean negative control,
establishing that the near-unity $L$ of the regular packings reflects genuine
flow-geometry locking rather than a trivial artefact of the de-rotation procedure.

\subsection{Is the Locking Genuine? Discriminating Controls}
\label{sec:locking:control}

Because every layer is de-rotated into a common frame before $L$ is evaluated, it is
natural to ask whether the near-unity values are a genuine physical result or are partly
manufactured by the de-rotation procedure itself. Two built-in controls show that they are
genuine.

First, the de-rotation cannot create similarity where none exists. The irregular packing
is processed through the \emph{identical} pipeline-each layer de-rotated by its own
angle and interpolated onto the same grid-yet its layers do not coincide
($\overline{L}=0.49$; Table~\ref{tab:control}). If de-rotation mechanically forced high
similarity it would do so here too; it does not. The irregular packing is therefore the
negative control for the metric, not merely for the physics.

Second, de-rotation does not make different flows look alike. Comparing the de-rotated
fields \emph{across} topologies on a common grid yields only moderate similarity-$L=0.69$
between $30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$, and $0.44$ between $30^\circ$ and the irregular
packing-far below the $0.998$ found \emph{within} the $30^\circ$ packing
(Table~\ref{tab:control}). The $30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ packings resemble each other
partially because both organise into coherent slit jets, but they remain clearly
distinguishable. A procedure that trivially inflated $L$ would drive all of these toward
unity; instead, high $L$ arises only when genuinely similar fields are compared.

\subsection{Locking Versus Reynolds Number}
\label{sec:locking:reynolds}

The locked state is a property of the time-mean flow, and as $Re_P$ rises the growing
unsteadiness is expected to erode it (hypothesis~H3). This is assessed for the $30^\circ$
packing between $Re_P = 100$ and $Re_P = 300$ on two complementary measures: the
layer-to-layer uniformity of the \emph{mean} field, and the level and uniformity of the
\emph{fluctuation} field, the latter obtained directly from the resolved Reynolds-stress
tensor $\overline{u'_i u'_j}$ recorded during the time-averaging, through the turbulent
kinetic energy $k = \tfrac12\,\overline{u'_i u'_i}$.

The mean field weakens only mildly. The qualitative pattern persists-the same central
and slit jets remain locked to the slits-but the layer-to-layer uniformity of the mean
speed degrades: the coefficient of variation of the per-layer mean $|\bm u|$ at Pos\,3
rises from $1.4\%$ at $Re_P = 100$ to $4.9\%$ at $Re_P = 300$, a roughly three-fold spread
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tke}b).

The fluctuation field reveals the breakdown far more clearly (Fig.~\ref{fig:tke}). Two
effects compound. First, the fluctuation level rises sharply: the layer-averaged $k$
increases by almost an order of magnitude (from $0.062$ to $0.60\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$), so
that the ratio of fluctuation to mean-flow kinetic energy $k/k_{\rm mean}$ climbs from
$0.58$ at $Re_P = 100$-where the fluctuations already amount to roughly half the
mean-flow energy, reflecting unsteady wakes shed by the bars even in the nominally laminar
regime-to $1.03$ at $Re_P = 300$, where the fluctuations exceed the mean-flow energy.
Second, and more tellingly for locking, the fluctuations themselves \emph{de-lock}: the
layer-to-layer coefficient of variation of $k$ jumps from $2.2\%$ at $Re_P = 100$ to
$11.9\%$ at $Re_P = 300$. At the lower Reynolds number not only the mean flow but even the
turbulence statistics repeat from layer to layer (the $k/k_{\rm mean}$ curve in
Fig.~\ref{fig:tke} is essentially flat), whereas at $Re_P = 300$ the per-layer
fluctuation energy becomes markedly uneven. The fluctuation field, with its $5\times$
larger scatter, is thus a more sensitive early indicator of locking breakdown than the
mean field. Physically, locking is strongest when the flow is steady and spatially
periodic in both its mean and its fluctuations, and erodes first through the growth and
de-synchronisation of the unsteady component.

A systematic Reynolds sweep through the laminar-turbulent transition, together with the
hydraulic characterisation (pressure drop and friction factor) that this fluctuation
growth ultimately governs, requires the time-resolved periodic-domain simulations and is
the subject of the companion paper (Paper~2); here we establish only that locking is
strongest at low $Re_P$ and weakens as the flow energises, led by the fluctuation field.

\subsection{Mechanism of Flow Locking}
\label{sec:locking:mechanism}

The near-unity $L$ establishes \emph{that} the flow locks; the mechanism explains
\emph{why}. The key is momentum inheritance across the slit hand-off. A high-momentum jet
issuing from a slit in layer $n$ enters layer $n+1$ still carrying most of its streamwise
momentum; because the slits of layer $n+1$ are rotated by exactly $\theta$, the geometry
realigns the incoming jet onto the next slit rather than disrupting it. The jet is thus
\emph{handed off} from slit to slit, turning by $\theta$ per layer and returning to its
original orientation after a full period of $N$ layers. The recirculation cells, being
anchored to the fixed wake positions behind the bars, repeat with the same period. Locking
is therefore the steady end state of a self-consistent, geometry-guided transport pathway:
each layer reproduces the inlet condition that the next layer requires.

Three observations support this picture. First, locking is established almost immediately:
the per-layer statistics are already uniform from the third layer (L03) onward
(Figs.~\ref{fig:Ln}, \ref{fig:tke}), so the alignment length is short-of order a single
period-and the developed window L13-L17 is fully locked. Second, the topology dependence
follows directly: the $40^\circ$ packing hands the jet off across a larger angle, demanding
more transverse momentum redistribution per layer and slightly degrading the inheritance
($L \approx 0.99$), while the irregular packing presents each layer with an uncorrelated
slit pattern that breaks up rather than realigns the incoming jet, severing the inheritance
chain entirely ($L = 0.10$-$0.68$). Third, the Reynolds-number trend is explained by where
the fluctuations live: the resolved $k$ concentrates in the jet shear layers and bar wakes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tkefield}), i.e.\ precisely at the hand-off interfaces between layers. As
$Re_P$ rises these fluctuations intensify and de-synchronise (Sec.~\ref{sec:locking:reynolds}),
progressively scrambling the hand-off and eroding the locked state from the fluctuation
field first.

\subsection{Physical Interpretation and Hand-off to Paper~2}
\label{sec:locking:handoff}

Physically, the locked state is a set of coherent jets that spiral upward in lock-step
with the rotating slits, reproducing the same pore-scale flow in every layer of a period.
This has a direct consequence for the modelling of the bed. If the developed mean flow is
fully described by the field within a single $N$-layer period-as the near-unity $L$
demonstrates for the regular packings-then the full-height apparatus is informationally
redundant: a reduced domain comprising one geometric period with periodic end conditions
should reproduce the interior flow of the complete bed. This is precisely the premise
exploited in Paper~2, where the locking established here is used to justify a reduced
six-layer ($30^\circ$) periodic domain for the friction-law and laminar-turbulent
transition study, at a small fraction of the full-apparatus cost. The irregular packing,
which does not lock, admits no such reduction and must be simulated in full-a limitation
that is itself a physical result of this section.

\subsection{Mean Flow Picture}
\label{sec:organisation:meanflow}

Having established that the flow locks, we now describe what the locked flow looks like.
The de-rotated mean fields of Fig.~\ref{fig:lockvis} reveal a strongly banded structure:
the flow organises into near-vertical high-velocity jets that thread through the slit
openings, separated by slower regions occupied by recirculation pockets in the wakes of
the bars. Because the slits rotate by $\theta$ from one layer to the next, these jets are
not stationary columns but spiral upward through the bed, tracing the screw-like pore
network; the de-rotation simply unwinds this spiral and exposes the underlying repeating
unit. At Pos\,3 the field is dominated by a central jet flanked by a symmetric pair of
slit jets (Fig.~\ref{fig:symmetry}), while at Pos\,1 the same banded organisation appears
shifted across the plane. The picture is consistent across all fifteen layers, which is
simply the visual signature of the locking quantified in Sec.~\ref{sec:locking}: a single
coherent jet-recirculation pattern, repeated layer after layer.

\subsection{Channelling and Transverse Spreading}
\label{sec:organisation:channelling}

Two scalar measures summarise how the kinetic energy is distributed within a plane. The
channelling index $\phi = \langle|\bm u|\rangle^2 / \langle|\bm u|^2\rangle$ (a
participation ratio over the fluid area) equals unity for a perfectly uniform field and
falls toward zero as the flow concentrates into ever-narrower jets. The transverse
kinetic-energy fraction $f_\perp = \langle u_{x'}^2\rangle / \langle|\bm u|^2\rangle$
quantifies how much of the in-plane energy resides in motion across the bed rather than
along it.

For the $30^\circ$ packing these measures confirm a moderately channelled flow with
substantial cross-stream motion. At $Re_P = 100$, Pos\,3, $\phi = 0.66$ and
$f_\perp = 18\%$: roughly two-thirds of the plane participates in the mean transport and
about a fifth of the in-plane energy is transverse, reflecting the spreading imparted by
the rotating slits. The values are robust across the plane (Pos\,1: $\phi = 0.61$,
$f_\perp = 31\%$) and shift only weakly with Reynolds number (Pos\,3, $Re_P = 300$:
$\phi = 0.68$, $f_\perp = 20\%$), the slight rise in $f_\perp$ indicating marginally more
cross-flow redistribution as the flow energises. The persistently large transverse
fraction distinguishes this rotated packing from a conventional aligned column, in which
the flow would be predominantly streamwise.

\subsection{Recirculation and Secondary Motion}
\label{sec:organisation:recirculation}

The in-plane streamlines of the mean field (Figs.~\ref{fig:lockvis} and~\ref{fig:symmetry})
reveal organised secondary motion between the jets. In the low-velocity regions flanking
each jet the streamlines close into recirculation cells seated in the wakes of the bars,
where the fluid driven aside by one slit jet is returned along the bar face. These cells
are a persistent feature of every layer, occupying the same positions relative to the jets
and confirming that the secondary motion is locked together with the primary flow rather
than fluctuating independently. Their rotational sense alternates across the plane in a
manner consistent with the point symmetry verified in Sec.~\ref{sec:numerics:symmetry}.
This secondary circulation is the mechanism behind the appreciable transverse
kinetic-energy fraction reported above, and it is the pore-scale stirring that a
rotationally stacked bed is intended to promote. Quantitative vortex diagnostics
(swirling strength, $Q$-criterion) are not pursued here, as the time-mean streamline
topology already characterises the recirculation adequately for the present purpose.

\subsection{Topology Contrast: 30\textdegree{} vs 40\textdegree{} vs Irregular}
\label{sec:organisation:contrast}

The three topologies organise their flow in markedly different ways, summarised in
Fig.~\ref{fig:topocontrast}, which compares the mean velocity-magnitude fields and
streamlines at Pos\,3, $Re_P = 100$.
The $30^\circ$ packing (Fig.~\ref{fig:topocontrast}a) produces the most coherent
organisation: sharply defined, near-parallel slit jets separated by orderly recirculation
cells, the banded structure that underpins its near-unity locking.
The $40^\circ$ packing (Fig.~\ref{fig:topocontrast}b) retains organised jets but at the
larger twist angle they are more strongly inclined and the streamlines show more
cross-flow redistribution between layers, with more prominent swirl cells-consistent
with its slightly reduced locking ($L \approx 0.99$) and the larger transverse spreading
expected of the steeper twist.
The irregular packing (Fig.~\ref{fig:topocontrast}c) is qualitatively different: the flow
collapses to a single dominant channel surrounded by disorganised streamlines and
scattered vortices of no fixed position, with no repeating banded structure. This erratic
transport is the spatial counterpart of its collapsed locking parameter
(Sec.~\ref{sec:locking:topologies}): without a geometric period there is no mechanism to
organise the jets layer by layer.
