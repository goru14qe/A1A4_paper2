\begin{abstract}
Rotationally stacked packed beds tailor the pore geometry through a fixed inter-layer rotation. The effect of this rotation on the pore-scale flow is not well understood. Earlier experiments observed that the flow appears to repeat from layer to layer, but did not explain this behaviour or test how it depends on the Reynolds number. Here, an experimentally validated lattice Boltzmann model of the complete bounded reactor is applied to three packings: two rotationally periodic configurations ($30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$) and an irregular control. Simulations are carried out at pore Reynolds numbers $Re_P=100$ and $300$. The predicted jet positions, widths, peak velocities, and recirculation zones agree with refractive-index-matched particle image velocimetry in the developed region. We then introduce \emph{flow locking}, defined as the recurrence of the time-mean pore-scale flow once the prescribed geometric rotation between layers is removed. A locking metric based on the spatial correlation of the de-rotated velocity fields quantifies its strength. Both periodic packings lock almost perfectly at $Re_P=100$ ($\overline{L}\approx0.99$). The irregular control does not ($\overline{L}=0.46$). Rotational periodicity is therefore required for a locked flow state. A higher Reynolds number weakens locking, and the rate depends on the topology. The $30^\circ$ packing stays strongly locked at $Re_P=300$ ($\overline{L}=0.959$), whereas the $40^\circ$ packing loses coherence ($\overline{L}=0.632$). Flow locking thus explains the recurring flow structures seen in experiments and establishes rotational topology as a design variable for pore-scale transport in structured packed-bed reactors.

\section{Introduction}
\label{intro}

Packed beds are widely used as catalytic reactors, adsorbers, and heat and mass-transfer devices in chemical, energy, and environmental process engineering~\citep{Dixon,Aglave}. Their performance including conversion, selectivity, pressure drop, and thermal management is governed not only by the bulk porosity but also by the organization of the pore-scale flow, which determines local residence times and interphase heat and mass transfer~\citep{Augier,freund2003numerical}. Given the widespread use of packed beds and the strong influence of pore-scale transport on their overall performance, accurate modeling of the underlying flow physics is essential. Conventional continuum models, such as those based on Darcy's law or volume-averaging techniques, represent the porous medium using effective transport coefficients rather than explicitly resolving the pore geometry. While these approaches are computationally efficient and suitable for macroscopic predictions, they are inherently unable to capture critical pore-scale flow phenomena, including jetting, channeling, and recirculation, which govern interstitial mixing. Consequently, resolving the flow at the scale of pores is essential for understanding the underlying transport mechanisms and, ultimately, for designing porous beds with enhanced performance.

Conventional beds of randomly poured particles offer little control over the pore-scale flow: the void network is statistically reproducible but geometrically uncontrolled, so the flow organisation cannot be tuned deliberately. 
Structured and additively manufactured packings change this premise by fixing the pore geometry by design, turning the internal topology into an engineering variable~\citep{Larsson.2018,li2022visualization}. The rotationally stacked, modular bed studied here is a deliberate instance of this idea: identical bar modules are stacked and each successive layer is rotated by a fixed angle relative to the one below, so that a single rotation increment acts as a design knob that reshapes the entire interstitial pore network. When the rotation angle is commensurate with the $180^\circ$ symmetry of the bar pattern, the stack becomes geometrically periodic over a finite number of layers (six layers at $30^\circ$, nine at $40^\circ$) and the pores trace a spiral path through the bed; an aperiodic, irregularly rotated stack provides a geometrically disordered counterpart.

This configuration has been experimentally characterized by means of optically corrected, refractive-index-matched particle image velocimetry, which resolves the time-averaged velocity field within the slits of the upper layers~\citep{velten2026,Martins.2018,velten2022a}; related PIV and tomographic studies have established such measurements as a reliable pore-scale reference in both random and ordered packings~\citep{Patil.2013,Khayamyan.2017,Poelma.2020}. A recurring qualitative observation in previous studies is that, in the periodically rotated beds, the the interstitial flow field appears to repeat from one geometric period to the next: successive layers seem to carry the same interstitial flow pattern, merely rotated with the geometry\citep{demir2026pressure}. However, this apparent layer-to-layer periodicity has been noted in passing but never defined, quantified, or tested against a disordered control, and it has not been examined across more than one rotation\citep{sadowski2025mean}.

Beyond these macroscopic observations, however, the underlying organisation of the pore-scale flow remains largely unexplored. Although successive layers were observed to exhibit similar velocity fields rotated with the packing geometry, this behaviour has only been noted qualitatively. Its physical origin, robustness across different rotational topologies, and distinction from the behaviour of non-periodic packings have not yet been investigated systematically.
Despite recent progress, the influence of rotational topology on pore-scale flow organisation remains poorly understood.In particular, it remains unclear whether these recurring flow structures are an intrinsic consequence of the imposed geometric periodicity or arise from other features of the packing arrangement.

The Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) has emerged as a promising computational approach for solving complex fluid flow problems. In recent years, LBM has been increasingly employed in multiphysics and challenging applications, including particle-laden flow simulations \cite{KRAUSE2017,Khalili2017, RETTINGER2018, GHARIBI2024,Gharibi2025}, multiphase flow modelling \cite{Mazloomi2015,Liu2016,gharibi2020,fei2020,GHARIBI2024-2, wu2025}, and fluid transport in porous media \cite{PAN2006, GHARIBI2020-2,STOCKINGER2024,Fei2023, ZHOU2026}. In particular, its robustness in resolving pore-scale flow within complex porous structures has been demonstrated. The growing popularity of LB methods can be attributed to several advantages, including the simplicity of the underlying numerical scheme, highly scalable parallel implementation, the absence of the need to solve an elliptic Poisson equation for pressure correction, and the straightforward treatment of complex and irregular geometries. Leveraging these capabilities, an experimentally validated lattice Boltzmann model is employed in this work to investigate whether the recurring pore-scale flow structures observed in periodically stacked packings originate from the underlying rotational topology. The similarity of flow fields between successive layers is quantified, and the resulting flow organisation in periodic and irregular packing configurations is compared.

To address these questions, the present work combines pore-scale lattice Boltzmann simulations~\citep{kruger2017lattice,Sullivan} with particle image velocimetry measurements for three packing topologies: the periodic $30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ stacks and an irregular configuration. The simulations reproduce the complete bounded experimental apparatus and are validated against layer-resolved velocity measurements. Building on this validated framework, flow locking is explored as the recurrence of pore-scale flow structures imposed by the underlying packing topology. It is quantified using an objective similarity metric, and its dependence on topology and Reynolds number is examined. Comparisons between periodic and irregular packings are used to establish the role of rotational periodicity in organizing pore-scale flow structures and to provide a physical basis for the use of reduced periodic computational domains.

\subsection{Rotationally Periodic Packing}
\label{sec:geometry:topology}

Because the modules are identical and only their angular offset changes, the bed is generated by a single \emph{rotational periodicity} rule: each module is the one below it, rotated about the column axis by a fixed angle $\theta$ and shifted up by one layer height $l_\mathrm{ch}$. The structure therefore possesses two competing geometric length scales. The first is the per-layer misalignment $\theta$, which controls how far an open slit (and hence a through-flowing jet) stays aligned before it is occluded by the bars of the next module. The second is a superlattice period: since the bar pattern of a single module is invariant under a $180^\circ$ rotation, the stack returns to its original orientation after
\begin{equation}
    N = \frac{180^\circ}{\theta}
    \label{eq:period}
\end{equation}
layers. For the regular $30^\circ$ configuration ($\theta = 30^\circ$) gives $N = 6$, so six successive modules span and the seventh module repeats the orientation of the first~\citep{velten2026}. The $40^\circ$ configuration gives the longer period $N = 9$. The full bed of $18$ stacked modules (bed height $18\,l_\mathrm{ch} = 180\,\mathrm{mm}$) thus contains exactly three complete geometric periods of the $30^\circ$ packing, producing the spiral inner pore structure illustrated in \hl{Fig(to be added)}.~\ref{fig:geometry}. All optical measurements~\citep{velten2026}, and \hl{the simulation planes (really??) }used for validation here, are taken in layers L13-L17, i.e.\ within the third (fully developed) period counted from the gas inlet at the bottom of the bed.

A consequence of the $180^\circ$ symmetry of the bar pattern is that the inner pore
network is point-symmetric between two layers: when two modules are rotated against each other, the first and fourth voids carry the same number of inlets as the second and third, and depending on $\theta$ between one and four through-passages connect a lower module to the one above it~\citep{velten2026}. The $30^\circ$ configuration realises a $2/3$ inlet pattern.

\subsection{Topology Configurations}
\label{sec:geometry:topologies}

The modularity of the reactor allows a continuum of pore structures to be assembled
from the same hardware simply by choosing the inter-layer rotation angle~\citep{velten2026}. We study three representative topologies here:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$\theta = 30^\circ$ (regular, period $N = 6$):} the experimentally measured benchmark configuration, with the shortest period and the longest jet-alignment length of the cases considered.
    \item \textbf{$\theta = 40^\circ$ (regular, period $N = 9$):} a second periodic packing whose larger per-layer rotation shortens the alignment length and lengthens the geometric period.
    \item \textbf{Irregular (aperiodic control):} successive layers are rotated by a non-repeating sequence of angles, so there is no geometric periodicity rule and no
    superlattice period. This packing serves as a clean control: it shares the same bars, slits, porosity and bounding apparatus as the regular cases but lacks the geometric periodicity, and therefore cannot exhibit flow locking (Sec.~\ref{sec:locking}).
\end{itemize}

Simulations at $Re_P = 100$ and $Re_P = 300$ are performed for all three packing topologies ($30^\circ$, $40^\circ$, and the irregular configuration), as corresponding PIV measurements are available for these conditions and provide the primary validation cases (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}).

\section{Numerical methods}
\label{sec:numerics}
This section describes the numerical methods employed to investigate pore-scale fluid flow in packed beds and the methodology used for flow-locking analysis. First, the enhanced central-moment Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM), which efficiently satisfies the momentum balance for incompressible flows, as described in \cite{}, is presented. Subsequently, the flow-locking analysis used to quantify the recurrence of pore-scale flow structures between successive packing layers is described.
\subsection{Lattice Boltzmann Formulation (ALBORZ)}
\label{sec:numerics:lbm}

The flow simulations presented here are performed using the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM), which may be regarded as a discretization on the kinetic-scale of the Boltzmann equation that recovers the Navier-Stokes equations in the hydrodynamic limit~\cite{Xiaoyi}. The numerical solver and formulation adopted here are identical to those previously developed and validated against packed-bed experiments by the present author~\cite{neeraj2023}; readers are referred to that work for a comprehensive description. Briefly, the method begins with a projection of the Boltzmann equation onto a discrete velocity space, whereby the particle distribution function is approximated through a truncated Hermite polynomial expansion. Integration of the resulting system of coupled hyperbolic equations along their characteristic trajectories~\cite{Bhadauria} yields the well-known two-step stream-and-collide algorithm:
\begin{equation}
    f_i\left(\bm{r}+\bm{c}_i\delta t, t+\delta t\right) - f_i\left(\bm{r}, t\right) = \Omega_i,
\end{equation}
Here the $f_i$ denote the discrete populations advected along the lattice links with velocities $\bm{c}_i$, $\delta t$ is the discrete time increment, and $\Omega_i$ is the collision operator, which drives the populations towards their equilibrium state in mimicry of inter-molecular collisions. For weakly non-equilibrium flows i.e.\ at the low Knudsen numbers characteristic of the present configuration it is common to adopt the single-relaxation-time Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) closure, whose discrete counterpart reads:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i = \omega\left( f^{\rm eq}_i - f_i\right) + \Xi_i,
\end{equation}
in which $\omega$ sets the rate of relaxation towards equilibrium and $f^{\rm eq}_i$ is the discretised equilibrium attractor, i.e.\ the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution recovered as the entropy-minimising state~\cite{Bhadauria,Shu}. The additional term $\Xi_i$ is a correction that compensates for the spurious deviations arising in the diagonal entries of the third-order equilibrium moments whenever a third-order quadrature is used. The equilibrium itself is constructed, as is standard in the method, by truncating the Hermite expansion of the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution at finite order and evaluating it on the Gauss-Hermite quadrature nodes:
\begin{equation}
    f^{\rm eq}_i = w_i \sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{n!c_s^{2n}} \mathcal{H}_n(\bm{c}_i):a_n^{\rm eq}(\rho,\bm{u},p/\rho),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}_n$ denotes the rank-$n$ Hermite polynomial tensor, $a_n^{\rm eq}$ its associated equilibrium coefficient, $w_i$ the quadrature weights and $c_s$ the lattice sound speed fixed by the quadrature; $\rho$, $\bm{u}$ and $p$ are the density, velocity and pressure, and $N$ the truncation order. Throughout this work we employ the D3Q27 lattices ($D$ the spatial dimension, $Q$ the number of links), both of which correspond to a third-order quadrature; the expansion is therefore truncated at $N=4$ in two dimensions and $N=6$ in three, these being the highest orders the respective stencils can support. A virtue of this higher-order construction, in contrast with the conventional second-order equilibrium, is that the shear-mode dissipation rate retains Galilean invariance at the Navier-Stokes level. The complementary correction $\Xi_i$ extends this invariance to the dissipation of the normal modes and is given by~\cite{hosseini2020compressibility,feng2015three}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:correction}
    \Xi_i = \left(1-\frac{\omega}{2}\right)\frac{w_i}{2c_s^4}\left(\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{H}_2\right):\Delta^{\rm eq}_3,
\end{equation}
with $\Delta^{\rm eq}_3$ the rank-three diagonal tensor
\begin{equation}
    \Delta^{\rm eq}_{\alpha\alpha\alpha} = \rho u_\alpha \left(u_\alpha^2 + 3p/\rho\right) - \sum_i c_{i,\alpha}^3 f^{\rm eq}_i.
\end{equation}
In practice the plain BGK operator is frequently replaced by more elaborate collision models, motivated either physically (e.g.\ to recover an arbitrary Prandtl number) or numerically (to enhance stability). A prominent example is the multiple-relaxation-time (MRT) approach, in which the relaxation is performed not on the populations themselves but on a set of independent base functions usually moments of the distribution and which has gained wide adoption thanks to its favourable stability and accuracy. The collision model used here is an MRT operator formulated in a basis of modified central Hermite moments, which in particular permits the bulk viscosity to be tuned independently~\cite{hosseini2022lattice}. Unlike the classical Hermite-moment basis~\cite{hosseini2021central,huang2022simulation}, this choice lets the trace and the deviatoric parts of the second-order moments relax with distinct rates:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_i = \mathcal{T}^{-1}W\mathcal{T}(f^{\rm eq}_i - f_i) + \Xi_i\label{eq:correc1}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}$ maps the populations into moment space, $\mathcal{T}^{-1}$ is its inverse and $W$ collects the relaxation rates on its diagonal. The transform $\mathcal{T}$ is built from the modified central Hermite polynomials $\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_n(\bm{c}_i)$, which for the D2Q9 lattice take the form:\hl{(Our work is three-dimensional, and a 2D solver was not used in this study!!!)}
\begin{subequations}
	\begin{align}
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_0 &= 1, \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_x &= c_{i,x}-u_x, \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_y &= c_{i,y}-u_y, \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xy} &= (c_{i,x}-u_x) (c_{i,y}-u_y), \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xx-yy} &= {(c_{i,x}-u_x)}^2 - {(c_{i,y}-u_y)}^2, \\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xx+yy} &= {(c_{i,x}-u_x)}^2 + {(c_{i,y}-u_y)}^2 - 2c_s^2,\\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xxy} &= {(c_{i,x}-u_x)}^2(c_{i,y}-u_y) - c_s^2(c_{i,y}-u_y),\\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xyy} &= {(c_{i,y}-u_y)}^2(c_{i,x}-u_x) - c_s^2(c_{i,x}-u_x),\\
	\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{xxyy} &= \left[{(c_{i,x}-u_x)}^2 - c_s^2\right] \left[{(c_{i,y}-u_y)}^2 - c_s^2\right].
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Projecting the equilibrium onto this basis yields the corresponding central Hermite coefficients $\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_n$, which for the D2Q9 equilibrium are:
\begin{subequations}
	\begin{align}
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_0 &= \rho, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_x &= 0, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_y &= 0, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xy} &= 0, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xx-yy} &= 0, \\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xx+yy} &= 2(p - \rho c_s^2),\\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xxy} &= 0,\\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xyy} &= 0,\\
	\widetilde{a}^{\rm eq}_{xxyy} &= \rho{(p/\rho - c_s^2)}^2.
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
For the particular choice $p=\rho c_s^2$ every coefficient above vanishes except the zeroth-order one. On the D2Q9 lattice the diagonal relaxation tensor $W$ is assembled as:
\begin{equation}
    W={\rm diag}(1, 1, 1, \omega_s, \omega_s, \omega_b, \omega_g, \omega_g, \omega_g),
\end{equation}
where the operator ${\rm diag}$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    {\rm diag}(\bm{A}) = (\bm{A}\otimes\bm{1})\circ \bm{I},
\end{equation}
in which $\bm{A}$ is an arbitrary vector, $\bm{1}$ the all-ones vector, $\bm{I}$ the identity tensor and $\circ$ the Hadamard (element-wise) product. The entries $\omega_s$, $\omega_b$ and $\omega_g$ are the relaxation frequencies of the shear, bulk and ghost modes, respectively. The bulk-mode frequency $\omega_b$ is tied to the bulk viscosity $\eta$ through:
\begin{equation}
    \omega_b = \frac{\delta t}{(\frac{2+D}{D}-\frac{\partial\ln p}{\partial\ln \rho})\frac{\eta}{p} + \delta t/2}.
\end{equation}
Within this multi-relaxation framework the third-order correction $\Xi_i$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:correc1} is recast accordingly, the single frequency $\omega$ being replaced by the harmonic combination of the shear and bulk rates:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:correction_MRT}
    \Xi_i = \left(1-\frac{\omega_b\omega_s}{\omega_b+\omega_s}\right)\frac{w_i}{2c_s^4}\left(\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{H}_2\right):\Delta^{\rm eq}_3.
\end{equation}
Taken together, the formulation outlined above delivers a freely adjustable bulk viscosity while preserving the Galilean invariance of both the dynamic and the bulk viscosities.

\subsection{Computational Domain and Boundary Conditions}
\label{sec:numerics:domain}

While a variety of different boundary conditions have been developed for the lattice Boltzmann method, in the context of the present study all solid boundaries are modeled using the half-way bounce-back scheme where missing populations after the collision-streaming steps are computed as~\cite{kruger2017lattice}:
\begin{equation}
    f_\alpha\left(\bm{x},t+\delta_t\right) = f^{*}_{\bar{\alpha}}\left(\bm{x},t\right),
\end{equation}
where $f^{*}$ is the post-collision population (prior to streaming) and $\bar{\alpha}$ is the index of the particle velocity opposite that of $\alpha$. To get rid of the stair-case approximation the half-way bounce-back is supplemented with the treatment for curved boundaries as proposed in~\mbox{\cite{bouzidi2001momentum}}.

At a given boundary node $\bm{x}_f$, the missing incoming populations are computed as:
\begin{subequations}
	\begin{align}
		f_\alpha(\bm{x}_f, t+\delta_t) &= 2qf_{\bar{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_f+\bm{c}_{\bar{\alpha}}, t+\delta_t)\nonumber \\  &+\left(1-2q\right)f_{\bar{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_f, t+\delta_t), \quad \forall\; q<\frac{1}{2},\\
		f_\alpha(\bm{x}_f, t+\delta_t) &= \frac{1}{2q}f_{\bar{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_f+\bm{c}_{\bar{\alpha}}, t+\delta_t)\nonumber \\  &+\frac{2q-1}{2q}f_{\bar{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_f, t+\delta_t), \quad \forall\; q\geq\frac{1}{2},
		\end{align}
	\label{Eq:CE_moments_eq}
\end{subequations}
where $\bar{\alpha}$ designates the direction opposite $\alpha$ and $q$:
\begin{equation}
    q = \frac{\lvert\lvert \bm{x}_f - \bm{x}_s\lvert\lvert}{\lvert\lvert\bm{c}_\alpha \lvert\lvert},
\end{equation}
with $\bm{x}_s$ denoting the wall position in direction $\alpha$.

Other boundary conditions applied at the inlets and outlets, i.e.\ constant velocity and constant pressure, are realized using the non-equilibrium extrapolation approach proposed in~\cite{zhao2002non}. In this approach the missing populations at the boundary nodes are reconstructed as:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\alpha}\left(\bm{x}_w,t+\delta_t\right) = f^{(eq)}_{\alpha}\left(\bm{x}_w,t+\delta_t\right) + f^{(neq)}_{\alpha}\left(\bm{x}_w,t+\delta_t\right),
\end{equation}
where $\bm{x}_w$ is the position of the boundary node and $f^{(neq)}$ the non-equilibrium part of the distribution function. One of the two variables (density or velocity) is known via the boundary condition and the other is computed by interpolation from neighboring nodes. The non-equilibrium part of the distribution function is also computed via an interpolation in space.

\subsection{Locking Order Parameter \texorpdfstring{$L(n)$}{L(n)}}
\label{sec:locking:Ln}

To quantify the recurrence of pore-scale flow between successive layers, we introduce a \emph{flow-locking metric}. While flow locking is defined in the present work as the recurrence of the time-averaged flow after accounting for the prescribed geometric rotation, the similarity measure itself is based on the normalised spatial inner product widely used in velocity-field analysis and proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)~\citep{lumley1967,sirovich1987,berkooz1993pod}. The novelty therefore lies not in the mathematical formulation itself, but in its application to de-rotated pore-scale velocity fields as an objective measure of topology-induced flow recurrence.

Rather than comparing different realisations of a flow, as is common in spatial correlation or POD analyses, the present metric compares successive layers within the same packing and interprets their similarity as a measure of topology-induced flow organisation.

The similarity between the de-rotated mean velocity fields of layers $m$ and $n$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
L_{mn}
=
\frac{\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}_m,\tilde{\bm{u}}_n\rangle_{\Omega_{mn}}}
{\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_m\|_{\Omega_{mn}}
\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_n\|_{\Omega_{mn}}},
\qquad
\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle_{\Omega}
=
\sum_{i\in\Omega}
\left(
a_{x',i} b_{x',i}
+
a_{z',i} b_{z',i}
\right),
\label{eq:Ln}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\bm{u}}_n=(\tilde u_{x'},\tilde u_{z'})_n$ is the time-averaged velocity field of layer $n$ after de-rotation into the common reference frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry:coords}), $\|\bm a\|_\Omega=\sqrt{\langle\bm a,\bm a\rangle_\Omega}$, and $\Omega_{mn}$ denotes the fluid region common to both layers. The normalisation removes the influence of velocity magnitude, such that $L_{mn}=1$ corresponds to identical de-rotated velocity fields, while progressively smaller values indicate increasing differences in the pore-scale flow organisation. Evaluating Eq.~(\ref{eq:Ln}) for all layer pairs produces the layer-to-layer locking matrix.

Two scalar quantities summarise the locking behaviour. The mean locking strength,
\begin{equation}
\overline{L}
=
\frac{2}{N_L(N_L-1)}
\sum_{m<n}
L_{mn},
\label{eq:Lbar}
\end{equation}
is the average similarity between all distinct layer pairs and provides a single measure of the overall strength of flow locking. \hl{Here $N_L$ is ... . } Values of $\overline{L}$ approaching unity indicate that the de-rotated flow structures are nearly identical throughout the packing, whereas lower values correspond to progressively weaker locking.
The second quantity, the per-layer locking,
\begin{equation}
L(n)
=
\frac{\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}_n,\bar{\bm{u}}\rangle}
{\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_n\|
\|\bar{\bm{u}}\|},
\qquad
\bar{\bm{u}}
=
\frac{1}{N_L}
\sum_{m=1}^{N_L}
\tilde{\bm{u}}_m,
\label{eq:Lofn}
\end{equation}

measures the similarity of each individual layer to the mean de-rotated flow field. Unlike $\overline{L}$, which characterises the packing as a whole, $L(n)$ identifies local departures from the locked state and therefore highlights layers that deviate most strongly from the repeating flow pattern.

Each de-rotated field is interpolated onto a common $(x',z')$ grid, and comparisons are performed only over the fluid region shared by the corresponding layer pair. 

\section{Simulation Parameters}
\label{sec:numerics:params}

In this study, the working fluid is air at the temperature of $T = 20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and the pressure of $p = 1\,\mathrm{atm}$ with a density of $\rho = 1.204\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, and a dynamic viscosity of $\mu = 1.822\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{kg/(m\,s)}$. The operating conditions are characterized by the particle Reynolds number, defined as
\begin{equation}
    Re_P = \frac{\rho\,v_s\,l_\mathrm{ch}}{\varphi\,\mu},
    \label{eq:rep}
\end{equation}
where $l_\mathrm{ch}=10\,\mathrm{mm}$ is the characteristic bar width and $v_s$ is the superficial velocity \citep{velten2026}. The superficial velocity is obtained from the volumetric flow rate $Q$ via $Q = A\,v_s$, where $A = 3031\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ denotes the reactor cross-sectional area.
In this study three differen particle Reynolds number of $Re_P=100, 300, 500$ is considered. In the reference experiment $Re_P = 100$ correspond to $Q = 9.14$$\mathrm{L/min}$, $Re_P = 300$ correspond to $Q = 13.71$$\mathrm{L/min}$ and $Re_P = 500$ correspond to $Q = 45.72$$\mathrm{L/min}$~\citep{velten2026}.



The lattice is uniform and Cartesian. The decisive geometric length scale for the grid is the $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ slit width $l_P$, which is common to all three topologies; the production resolution places about $17$ nodes across each slit (and correspondingly $33$ across each $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ bar) at $Re_P = 100$, the medium resolution justified by the grid-independence study of Sec.~\ref{sec:numerics:grid}. The lattice spacing and time step are refined as the Reynolds number increases, both to keep the near-wall shear layers resolved and to maintain a low lattice Mach number, so that the slit is resolved by $33$ nodes at $Re_P = 300$. The transverse extent of the domain ($\approx 81\,\mathrm{mm}$) comfortably encloses the $d_\mathrm{inlet} = 62.12\,\mathrm{mm}$ bed cross-section, and the streamwise extent spans the $18$-module stack plus the upstream inlet section (Sec.~\ref{sec:numerics:domain}). \subsection{Measurement Positions and Coordinate System}
\label{sec:geometry:coords}
A right-handed global Cartesian system $(x,y,z)$ is fixed to the apparatus: $x$ is horizontal, the depth axis $y$ originates at the centre of the module, and the streamwise axis $z$ points upward with its origin at the bottom of the lowest module (Fig.~\ref{fig:coords}\citealt{velten2026}). Since each measurement (or extraction) plane lies in a void at a different rotation angle, an in-plane coordinate system is used in addition: it keeps the same vertical axis $z$ but replaces $x$ with the horizontal in-plane coordinate $\xi$, aligned with the open slit and zero at the void centre. The two directly reported velocity components are the horizontal in-plane velocity $v_\xi$ and the vertical (streamwise) velocity $v_z$.\\
A measurement plane is located in the global frame by its offset $a_{\mathrm{Pos},n}$ from the reactor centre, measured along $y$ for the $0^\circ$ orientation of the module; this offset is independent of the rotation angle. Four positions are defined (Table~\ref{tab:pos}). Because the bed is point-symmetric about the column axis (Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry:topology}), Pos\,1/Pos\,7 and Pos\,3/Pos\,5 form point-symmetric pairs; the experiment exploits this symmetry, measuring only at Pos\,1 and Pos\,3 to recover the full set of flow fields~\citep{velten2026}. The present validation correspondingly extracts simulation planes at Pos\,1 and Pos\,3.

Because the void in which each plane lies has been twisted by the per-layer rule (Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry:topology}), the raw simulation fields are first de-rotated into the common in-plane frame before any comparison or layer-to-layer analysis. Each layer $L$ is rotated about the vertical axis by
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = -\,(18 - L)\,\theta,
    \label{eq:derotation}
\end{equation}
so that the open slit of every layer is aligned with the in-plane axis $\xi$ (e.g.\ for the $30^\circ$ packing $\alpha = 0,\,-30^\circ,\,-60^\circ,\dots$ for $L = 18, 17, 16,\dots$; for the $40^\circ$ packing the step is $40^\circ$). The position coordinates $(x,y,z)$ are transformed by the planar rotation
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:transform}
\begin{align}
    x' &= \phantom{-}x\cos\alpha + y\sin\alpha, \\
    y' &= -x\sin\alpha + y\cos\alpha, \\
    z' &= z,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
and the velocity components $(v_x, v_y, v_z)$ are rotated consistently,
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:transform_vel}
\begin{align}
    v_x' &= v_x\cos\alpha + v_y\sin\alpha, \\
    v_y' &= v_x\sin\alpha + v_y\cos\alpha, \\
    v_z' &= v_z,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with the vertical coordinate and the streamwise velocity left unchanged. The reported in-plane velocity magnitude combines the horizontal in-plane and streamwise components within the plane,
\begin{equation}
    v_\mathrm{mag} = \sqrt{ {v_x'}^2 + {v_z'}^2 }.
    \label{eq:velmag}
\end{equation}
After this transformation the horizontal in-plane component $v_x'$ corresponds to $v_\xi$ and $v_z'$ to $v_z$ of the in-plane frame.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
This section presents a systematic assessment of the numerical framework in three stages. First, a grid-independence study is performed to establish the spatial resolution required for accurate predictions, followed by symmetry and consistency verification to further assess the reliability and robustness of the numerical approach. Second, the capability of the developed numerical tools to resolve flow behavior within the packing structures is demonstrated through validation against the experimental PIV measurements. Finally, a flow-locking analysis is conducted to investigate whether the pore network geometry induces repeatable flow patterns and to examine the influence of Reynolds number on the persistence and evolution of these structures.

\label{sec:numerics:symmetry}

However, presenting all planes for every Reynolds number would lead to a substantial amount of redundant data. Owing to the geometric arrangement of the slits, the measurement locations form two point-symmetric pairs about the column axis: Pos,1 and Pos,7, and Pos,3 and Pos,5 (Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry:coords}). Figure~\ref{fig:symmetry} compares the time-averaged velocity-magnitude contours and in-plane streamlines at Pos,3 and Pos,5 for the $30^\circ$ packing at $Re_P=100$ and $Re_P=300$. \subsection{Validation against PIV Measurements}\label{sec:validation}
In this study, the reliability of the numerical solver is assessed by validating the predicted flow behavior within the packing structures against experimental measurements.
The simulation results for all three packing topologies are compared with the published PIV data of~\citet{velten2026}. This validation section is organized into three stages. First, the comparison methodology and error metrics are introduced; second, the time-averaged velocity-magnitude fields on the measurement planes are assessed for surface validation; and finally, the layer-resolved development of the flow within the slits is examined.

\subsubsection{Comparison Procedure and Error Metrics}
\label{sec:validation:procedure}

Velocity data are reported in the same in-plane coordinate system as used by~\citet{velten2026} consisting of the horizontal in-plane direction $\xi$ and the vertical streamwise direction $z$. Simulation planes are extracted at the physical positions corresponding to Pos\,1 and Pos\,3 ($a_\mathrm{Pos} = -22.5$ and $-7.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the reactor centre). Each extracted plane is masked to the fluid region, interpolated onto the experimental PIV grid (vector resolution $3.40$-$3.44\,\mathrm{vec/mm}$, Table~2 of \citealt{velten2026}), and compared with the measured field point by point.

Agreement is quantified on the velocity magnitude $|\bm{v}|$ over all $M$ fluid grid points of a plane. Denoting the simulated and experimental values at point $i$ by $s_i$ and $e_i$, we report the root-mean-square error
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(s_i-e_i\right)^2},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{NRMSE} = \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}}{e_\mathrm{max}-e_\mathrm{min}},
    \label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
its value normalised by the experimental dynamic range ($\mathrm{NRMSE}$), and the coefficient of determination $R^2 = 1-\sum_i(s_i-e_i)^2/\sum_i(e_i-\bar{e})^2$, which measures how much of the spatial variance of the measured field is reproduced. $\mathrm{NRMSE}$ is the primary headline metric, as it is insensitive to the absolute velocity level and is therefore comparable across topologies and Reynolds numbers.

\subsubsection{Surface Velocity Fields}
\label{sec:validation:surface}

The simulation results are first validated at the level of the time-averaged surface velocity field, where the PIV measurements provide the highest spatial resolution and the dominant flow features can be directly compared. The aim is to establish that the model reproduces both the spatial organisation of the flow and the corresponding absolute velocity levels over the entire measurement plane.

Figures~\ref{fig:validation_surface_re100} and~\ref{fig:validation_surface_re300} compare the simulated and measured velocity-magnitude fields, with in-plane streamlines, for the $30^\circ$ packing at Pos\,3 under flow conditions of $Re_P=100$ and $Re_P=300$, respectively. At both Reynolds numbers the flow organizes into discrete streamwise jets issuing from the slits and separated by low-velocity wakes, with a strong central jet flanked by weaker slit jets. The simulation accurately reproduces the number, location, width, and peak magnitude of these jets, as well as the streamline topology of the
intervening recirculation zones. Notably, the agreement remains comparably strong at $Re_P=300$ and $Re_P=100$, showing that the model retains its predictive capability as the flow intensity increases. This agreement is further quantified by the line profiles in panel~(b) of each figure, which overlay the simulated and measured velocity-magnitude profiles at four streamwise heights for Pos\,3
against the experimental uncertainty band. The simulated profiles track the measured ones within or close to the band almost everywhere, accurately capturing both the jet peaks and the inter-jet minima.

This level of agreement is representative, across the regular packings the surface fields are reproduced consistently well at both Reynolds numbers,
with $\mathrm{NRMSE}$ of $5$-$9\%$ and $R^2$ up to $0.93$. The residual error is not distributed uniformly but concentrates in the thin, high-gradient shear layers bordering the jets, where sub-vector-spacing velocity gradients and PIV peak-locking are largest.

\subsubsection{Layer-Resolved Development}
\label{sec:validation:layers}

Beyond the surface fields, the measurements also resolve the flow within the individual packing layers, allowing the simulation to be validated layer by layer in the fully developed region. The
complete dataset includes in-slit velocity fields and profiles for each measured layer (L13-L17), packing topology, Reynolds number and measurement position. o avoid presenting a large number of contour plots, only two contrasting cases at $Re_P = 100$,t Pos\,3 are shown: the regular $30^\circ$ packing and the irregular packing. Together, these cases demonstrate both accuracy of the numerical method and the distinctly different flow development associated with the two packing arrangements.

As shown in Figure ~\ref{fig:validation_layers}, for the regular $30^\circ$ packing, there is a very good agreement between the simulated and measured velocity-magnitude fields. The velocity-magnitude contours and corresponding in-plane streamlines (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_layers}a) accurately reproduce the jet-recirculation structure observed in each layer.
The remaining discrepancies are primarily confined to thin shear layers at the jet boundaries, where the large velocity gradients amplify small differences between the numerical predictions and the PIV measurements. The per-layer line velocity-magnitude profiles extracted $2\,\mathrm{mm}$ above the lower face
(Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_layers}b) show that the five layers L13-L17 carry essentially the same profile. The simulation accurately reproduces the measured profile in each layer, capturing the central jet, the flanking secondary jets, and the inter-jet minima at the correct locations. A small, systematic offset is however visible: the simulated central peak and the secondary-jet maxima sit a few percent below the measured ones, while the inter-jet minima are correspondingly slightly higher. The two observations are linked and point to a single cause. The slit jets and their bounding shear layers are the steepest-gradient features in the domain, and each jet core is resolved by only a handful of lattice nodes across its width; there the leading truncation error of the scheme acts as a weak numerical dissipation that clips the tip of the jet. Because the total mass flux through the plane is
conserved, the momentum shaved from the peak is not lost but redistributed into the adjacent low-velocity region exactly the signature seen, lower maxima together with raised minima at unchanged jet position and width. A finer lattice would narrow this gap, but at a steep cost
in the present multi-module domain; the chosen resolution already lies in the grid-converged range for the integral quantities (Sec.~\ref{sec:numerics:grid}).
Another factor is the idealised model geometry-perfectly
smooth, aligned bars and a uniform inlet-differs slightly from the printed parts ($0.3\%$ tolerance) and diffuser-fed inlet of the experiment, which can sharpen the real channelling through individual slits. Secondly, the finite PIV interrogation window lightly smooths the measured field (measurement error). The discrepancy is therefore small, spatially confined to the jet maxima, sign-consistent across all five layers and hence also resonates the same in the surface data above. It is well within the combined experimental and numerical uncertainty and it leaves both the validation conclusion and the layer-to-layer repeatability that is the point of this figure intact.

Figure~\ref{fig:validation_layers_irreg} shows the corresponding validation results for the irregular packing, which exhibits fundamentally different flow behavior from the periodic configurations. Because each layer has a unique local bar arrangement, the corresponding jet and wake structures are also unique. Unlike the $30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ packings, the flow fields cannot be collapsed onto a common profile without superimposing unrelated features. For this reason, each layer is validated individually against its corresponding PIV measurements (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation_layers_irreg}b).

Despite the absence of layer collapse, the numerical model accurately reproduces the measured flow in every layer. The simulations capture the layer-specific jet locations, jet widths, peak velocities, and associated recirculation regions, demonstrating that the irregular packing is validated with the same methodology as the periodic cases. Although the agreement is slightly less precise than for the regular packings. The origin of these differences is geometric rather than numerical. In the periodic configurations, the repeating topology causes any small manufacturing or assembly imperfections to be replicated from one period to the next, so their influence is effectively averaged over the repeated flow structures. In contrast, every layer of the irregular packing possesses a distinct geometry, making the local flow considerably more sensitive to small variations in rotation angle and the manufacturing tolerance of each individual module (approximately $0.3\%$).
Since the flow is concentrated into isolated channels bounded by strong shear layers, even minor geometric deviations can produce noticeable local velocity differences between the experiments and the idealised numerical geometry.

These discrepancies are therefore expected and do not diminish the validity of the simulations. Rather, the pronounced differences between the layer-wise velocity profiles from L12 to L17 provide direct evidence that flow locking is absent in the irregular topology. This behaviour is also observed at $Re_P=300$, where the simulations again reproduce the distinct flow pattern of each layer in close agreement with the corresponding PIV measurements, confirming that the absence of flow locking is a robust geometric characteristic rather than a low-Reynolds-number effect.

\subsection{Flow Locking Framework}
\label{sec:locking}

This section introduces the central concept of the present work: \emph{flow locking}. Previous studies~\citep{velten2026} observed qualitatively that the pore-scale flow repeats from layer to layer, following the rotation of the packing geometry, but did not quantify this behaviour or examine its dependence on topology. Here, flow locking is defined as the recurrence of the time-averaged pore-scale flow after accounting for the prescribed geometric rotation and is quantified through a scalar locking order parameter.

Flow locking has important implications for both modelling and reactor design. If the developed flow repeats over each geometric period, the fully developed region of the packed bed can be represented by a single periodic module, providing the physical basis for reduced periodic-domain simulations~\citep{patankar1977periodic}. More generally, flow locking demonstrates that rotational topology organises pore-scale transport in a deterministic manner, with the degree of organisation depending on both geometry and Reynolds number.

The analysis is performed by de-rotating the mean velocity field of each layer by $-n\theta$ into a common reference frame. For a perfectly locked flow, the de-rotated layers collapse onto a single repeating pore-scale pattern, fully described by one geometric period ($N=180^\circ/\theta$). In contrast, the irregular packing provides a natural control, as its de-rotated layers possess no underlying periodicity.

This behaviour \hl{which behavior?} is illustrated qualitatively in Fig.~\ref{fig:topocontrast}. After de-rotation, the developed layers of the $30^\circ$ packing become nearly indistinguishable, each exhibiting the same central jet and symmetric slit jets. In the irregular packing, no such collapse is observed and each layer retains a distinct flow structure. To place these observations on a quantitative basis, the following section introduces a locking order parameter that measures the similarity between de-rotated velocity fields.

\subsubsection{Flow Locking in Periodic and Irregular Packings}
\label{sec:locking:topologies}

Figure~\ref{fig:Ln} shows Locking order parameter for the three topologies at $Re_P = 100$. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Ln}-a, following de-rotation, the locking metric remains nearly constant across all fully developed layer pairs in the $30^\circ$ packing, with values approaching unity ($\overline{L}=0.998$). The negligible variation in the locking metric indicates that successive geometric periods exhibit nearly identical pore-scale flow fields. This observation suggests that the time-averaged flow converges to a geometrically repeating state despite the complexity of the pore structure. The $40^\circ$ packing exhibits similarly strong locking ($\overline{L}=0.993$), demonstrating that this behaviour is a general consequence of rotational periodicity rather than a unique feature of a single topology.

The near-unity similarity values indicate that the rotational geometry repeatedly reorganises the flow into the same arrangement of jets and recirculation zones after each geometric period. Although individual jets accelerate, decelerate, and interact with bar wakes within a layer, the overall pore-scale transport pattern is reconstructed from one period to the next.

To verify that the observed locking originates from the packing topology rather than the comparison procedure, the same analysis was applied to the irregular configuration. In contrast to the periodic packings, the de-rotated layers exhibit little mutual similarity ($\overline{L}=0.46$), with each layer retaining a distinct flow structure. The collapse observed for the periodic packings therefore arises from the underlying rotational periodicity and not from the de-rotation or the similarity metric itself. The irregular packing thus provides a natural control that validates flow locking as a genuine topology-induced phenomenon.

\subsubsection{Flow Organisation}
\label{sec:organisation}

Having established the existence of flow locking, we now examine the pore-scale flow structures responsible for this behaviour. After de-rotation into a common reference frame, the mean velocity fields collapse onto a repeating pattern consisting of high-velocity jets passing through the slit openings and recirculation zones in the wakes of the bars. Although stationary in the de-rotated frame, these structures follow the layer-by-layer rotation of the packing and therefore trace spiral transport pathways through the bed. At Pos\,3, the flow is characterised by a dominant central jet flanked by two symmetric slit jets (Fig.~\ref{fig:topocontrast}), a pattern that is reproduced throughout the developed region.



The periodic packings organise the flow in distinctly different ways. The $30^\circ$ packing exhibits the most coherent structure, with well-defined slit jets and stable recirculation zones. Increasing the rotation angle to $40^\circ$ produces more inclined jets and enhanced transverse transport while preserving the overall periodic organisation. In contrast, the irregular packing loses this coherent structure entirely, with the flow concentrating into a dominant preferential channel surrounded by irregular recirculation. The progressive loss of coherent flow organisation is consistent with the reduction in flow locking, confirming that the recurring pore-scale flow structures are imposed by the rotationally periodic geometry.

The contrast between the $30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ topologies originates from the angle through which the flow must be redirected between successive layers. As the packing rotates by $\theta$, a jet leaving one slit must align with a differently oriented slit in the layer above. For the $30^\circ$ packing this redirection is relatively small, allowing the jet structure to be preserved from layer to layer and resulting in near-perfect flow locking ($\overline{L}=0.998$). Increasing the rotation to $40^\circ$ requires stronger transverse momentum redistribution, producing more inclined jets and stronger inter-layer shear layers. Although the flow remains highly organised at $Re_P=100$ ($\overline{L}=0.993$), these \hl{(What does 'these' refer to???)} larger velocity gradients make the flow more susceptible to instability as the Reynolds number increases (Sec.~\ref{sec:locking:reynolds}). The larger rotational increment therefore organises the flow less rigidly and reduces the robustness of the locked state.\hl{(Here, a discussion is introduced without presenting the results for higher Reynolds numbers.!!!)}

These findings demonstrate that flow locking is not merely a geometric consequence of the packing arrangement, but a topology-controlled transport mechanism. A strongly locked flow preserves coherent transport pathways through successive layers, leading to more predictable local residence times and interstitial transport. Conversely, weakening the locked state promotes transverse momentum exchange and mixing between neighbouring flow paths. The ability to strengthen or weaken flow locking through the packing topology therefore provides a potential route for tailoring pore-scale transport in structured packed-bed reactors, with direct implications for heat transfer, mass transfer, and catalytic reactor performance.

\section{Conclusion}

This work employed an experimentally validated lattice Boltzmann model of the complete bounded reactor to investigate pore-scale flow organisation in rotationally stacked packed beds. Very good agreement with refractive-index-matched PIV measurements confirmed that the simulations accurately reproduced the developed flow across the periodic and irregular packings. The validated framework was then used to investigate the underlying organisation of the pore-scale flow.

Building on the recurring flow structures observed experimentally by \cite{velten2026}, the present work identified flow locking as an intrinsic consequence of rotationally periodic packing topology. The proposed locking metric provided the first quantitative measure of this topology-controlled flow organisation, demonstrating that periodically stacked beds evolve towards a reproducible pore-scale flow state that does not exist in geometrically irregular packings.

The strength of flow locking decreases with increasing Reynolds number, although its robustness depends strongly on the rotational topology. The smaller rotational increment of the $30^\circ$ packing preserves a coherent locked state over the Reynolds-number range considered, whereas the $40^\circ$ packing loses coherence as stronger inter-layer flow redirection promotes earlier shear-layer instability. The locked state therefore deteriorates first through increasing flow fluctuations before changes become evident in the mean flow.

Beyond identifying a previously unquantified flow phenomenon, these findings establish rotational topology as a mechanism for organising pore-scale flow. The recurring flow structures observed experimentally are shown to arise from a topology-induced locked flow state rather than from a simple geometric resemblance between successive layers. Flow locking therefore provides a physical framework for understanding how rotational periodicity governs the organisation and persistence of pore-scale transport. It also provides a quantitative criterion for assessing when reduced periodic computational domains faithfully represent the developed flow and when this assumption begins to fail as Reynolds number increases. More broadly, the results demonstrate that rotational topology can be treated as a genuine design variable for controlling pore-scale flow organisation in structured porous media.

Several limitations delimit the scope of the present findings and motivate further work. The analysis is restricted to two rotationally periodic topologies ($30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$) and a single irregular control at $Re_P=100$ and $300$, so the angle- and Reynolds-number boundaries of the locked flow state remain to be established. Furthermore, the present study focuses exclusively on the hydrodynamic field. An important next step is to determine how flow locking influences scalar transport, mixing, and heat and mass transfer, and whether these processes can be deliberately tailored through rotational topology. Extending the framework across a broader range of geometries and operating conditions will provide a basis for topology-guided design of next-generation structured packed-bed reactors and additively manufactured porous architectures.
